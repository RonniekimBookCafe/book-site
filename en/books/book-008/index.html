Physical AI with RoboCo · QUAD-01 · WALK

From one W key press
to a twelve-joint step

A yellow body on four black legs. While you hold W, the quadruped in RoboCo lifts its diagonal legs in turn and walks forward; let go, and it settles back onto all four feet. Press a number key and the same robot sits and waves, stretches, or holds a handstand.

The book has you walk the robot first, then trace that step back one layer at a time: the gait sets a foot target for each leg, IK turns each target into three joint angles, and twelve ServoMotors make the motion. You change one value, walk again under the same conditions, and when the robot falls, you narrow down which layer to check, in order.

Questions this book answers

What does walking on legs need that wheels don't?

Which foot lifts first, where it lands, and how the other feet hold up the body. The book starts from three questions a wheeled robot never has to ask.

How does one W key become twelve joints?

Walk key → gait → foot path per leg → IK → joint angles → ServoMotor. You read a single step as five layers.

Same angle, so why do the left and right legs move differently?

You check joint axes, turning direction, and left-right mirroring on one leg mounted on a stand, then fix a leg that turns the wrong way.

When it falls, where do you look first?

Model and start file → Port → joint direction → coordinates → IK and joint limits → foot path. You use the console log to narrow it down, fix one condition, and walk again.

What you'll be able to do

  1. Walk a quadruped with the keyboard and pick its movesRun forward, back, turns in place, and side steps in WALK, plus nine performances in ACTION, and learn the difference between keys you hold and keys you press once.
  2. Read one leg's three joints and how all twelve servos connectCheck the HX, HY, and KN axes and their directions, how the FL, FR, HL, and HR legs pair with Ports 2–13, and the left-right mirroring settings.
  3. Shape a pose by moving foot targetsChange FOOT_X, FOOT_Y, and FOOT_Z one axis at a time, compare the IK angles with the position FK gives back, and filter out targets a leg can't reach.
  4. Change one value and compare fairlyVary body height, transition time, foot lift height, stride, and walking speed one at a time, and record distance, tilt, and number of falls.
  5. Finish and improve a walking missionStand → walk → turn → walk → stop in a target zone, repeated under the same conditions; then use the failures to change one thing and raise the completion rate.

The robot: three-joint legs × 4 = a twelve-joint quadruped

  • Four legs, twelve joints: each leg has HX (swings out to the side), HY (swings forward and back), and KN (the knee).
  • FL, FR, HL, HR: front left, front right, hind left, hind right, named from the direction the robot's face points, not from the screen.
  • A TextScreen on the body shows the current action, waiting state, and foot targets; the console logs lines such as [INPUT], [STATE], and [JOINT].
  • The BALANCE model carries an IMU that reads the body's roll, pitch, and yaw.

This is the entry book for the QUAD track. You don't need any wheeled-robot experience: you start from scratch with this one quadruped and widen your control step by step, from one joint → one leg → four legs → coordinates → IK → posture → gait.

How to read RoboCo results: RoboCo is a game-based robot sandbox with a physics simulation. Distances, tilt, and falls are observations under simulated conditions. Use them to learn how to check what changes when you change a value, not as measurements of real hardware.

Final mission: stand → walk → turn → walk → stop

The last chapter comes back to the same W, A, D, and X keys you started with. You fill in a mission card with a start line and a target zone first, then run it several times under the same conditions and record whether the robot walked the whole way, stayed on its feet, and held its stop pose inside the zone. From those records you pick one problem, such as wobbling right after a turn, change one value, and compare again.

To be clear: this mission is not autonomous walking with sensors judging the destination. A person presses the keys in sequence, and a person judges success by watching the screen. Choosing a gait from sensor input comes in QUAD-02.

One step beyond the book: extras you'll only find here

The book covers only what you can check inside RoboCo. This section looks at the question that comes after you close it: how does this experience connect to real quadruped robots? Use it to judge how far the book will take you before you buy.

1. Why a robot that walks on legs?

Wheels are fast and efficient on flat floors. Where the ground breaks up or gets rough (stairs, curbs, gravel), legs that can lift a foot and set it down somewhere else have the advantage. That is why quadrupeds keep finding work in places built for people, such as facility inspection, patrols, and research platforms. The price is that a legged robot has to decide at every moment which feet hold up the body, so there is far more to control than on a wheeled robot. That is why this book starts at the twelve joints.

2. What's the same as a real quadruped, and what's different

  • The same skeleton: three joints per leg, IK that turns a foot target into joint angles, two diagonal pairs of legs taking turns to hold the body, and an IMU reading body tilt. The ideas you change by hand in this book show up in any description of a real quadruped.
  • What real robots add: a motor and gearbox at every joint, control of force (torque) and not just angle, combining several sensors to estimate the body's state, choosing a gait from the terrain, and carrying a gait learned in simulation over to a real machine.
  • What RoboCo gives you: falling doesn't break anything, and you can restart under the same conditions as often as you like. It is a good place to build the habit of changing one value and comparing results, over and over, at no cost.

3. A look inside the practice code

The free ZIP Book_Q01_Robot contains eight start files and Q01_Lib, the author's teaching library (55 Python files). Its folders follow the same layers as the book.

The keys and default values are right there in the code. What the code doesn't contain is the order to run things in, what to watch, which value to change and by how much, what to compare it with, and how to read the result. That is the book's text and appendices.

4. What this book doesn't cover

Autonomous walking, where the robot sees obstacles with sensors and chooses its own gait, and learning-based decisions are in QUAD-02. Walking over to pick up and move objects with a robot arm is in QUAD-03. Real hardware issues such as wiring, power, heat, and communication delay aren't covered. QUAD-01 focuses on the ground all of those stand on: how twelve joint commands become one step.

Who this book is for

  • New to robots or code: PART 1 never opens the code; you just press keys to walk and perform. Math only shows up when it's needed, as a way to connect inputs and results.
  • High school students: see quadruped walking with your own eyes, learn how joints, coordinates, and control relate, and write an inquiry report from one-variable experiments.
  • University students and early majors: connect IK and FK for a three-joint leg, workspace and joint limits, gait cycle and leg order, and IMU/PID balance to what you see on screen and in the log.
  • Makers and developers: follow CONTROL PARAMETERS in the start files and the Q01_Lib/core and walk code, and change the gait yourself.
  • Teachers and training programs: run repeatable experiments and mission assessments with the same practice models and record tables.

About the author, Ronnie Kim

Ronnie Kim is a full-stack software engineer and senior SW/HW architect with more than 20 years in software development. Drawing on design and build work across back end and front end, he handles technical reviews, new feature design, technical decision-making, and AI and AX transformation projects.

Robotics has been his other long-running field. He has won awards at robotics competitions and has spent over 20 years studying robot structure and control, sensors, motors, actuators, and how software and hardware connect. Lately he researches the full structure of physical AI and robot systems, from inputs and sensors through state, decisions, control, actuators, and feedback, and turns it into hands-on learning material.

In teaching robotics, he favors running a finished robot first, observing it, changing control values, and comparing results over long stretches of theory or code.
